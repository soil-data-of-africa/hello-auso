# Using the AUSO platform

### What can I use AUSO for?

The platform provides access to soil information, maps, observations, and modelled soil properties for Africa. You can use these data to explore soil characteristics, compare areas, support research, inform planning and policy, and provide evidence for decisions about land and soil management.

### Can you provide a fertiliser recommendation for my field?

**Not directly.** The platform provides soil information that can support a fertiliser recommendation, but a reliable recommendation also depends on the crop, expected yield, management practices, climate, fertiliser type, and—where appropriate—recent field or laboratory measurements. The platform should therefore be considered a **source of evidence for decision-making, rather than a fertiliser recommendation service**.

### Can I use the platform to decide what crop to grow?

The platform can provide information about soil properties that are relevant to crop suitability, but soil is only one part of the picture. Climate, water availability, topography, crop requirements, management practices and socio-economic factors should also be considered.

### Can I use these data for a specific field?

The answer depends on the scale and uncertainty of the available data. Continental maps are useful for understanding **general soil conditions and spatial patterns**, but they may not capture variation within an individual field. For site-specific decisions, field observations and laboratory analyses may be needed.

### Can I download the data?

Datasets produced by ASDAC are freely downloadable under a CC-BY Attribution license. However the platform at times references external datasets, for which specific usage restrictions may apply, these restrictions are clearly mentioned on the dataset page.

### Can I use the data in a scientific publication?

Yes, subject to the licence and citation requirements associated with the dataset. We recommend citing both the dataset and the associated methodology/publication where applicable.



# Understanding soil properties

### What is soil texture?

Soil texture describes the relative amounts of **sand, silt and clay** in a soil. It is an important fundamental property because it influences water retention, drainage, aeration, nutrient storage and many aspects of soil management.

### How do I determine the texture of my soil?

There are several levels of accuracy:

* **Field assessment:** soil can be assessed by hand using simple texture-by-feel methods.
* **Simple tests:** sedimentation or jar tests can provide an approximate indication of sand, silt and clay content.
* **Laboratory analysis:** a laboratory can determine particle-size distribution using standard analytical methods and provides a more reliable result.

A field or jar test is useful for a first assessment, but it should not be considered equivalent to a laboratory measurement.

### How does soil texture affect soil quality?

Texture influences many important soil functions. **Sandy soils** generally drain rapidly but have relatively low water- and nutrient-holding capacity. **Clay-rich soils** can retain more water and nutrients but may have drainage, compaction or workability constraints. **Loams** often have a useful balance of these properties.

However, **texture alone does not determine soil quality**. Organic matter, structure, pH, nutrient status, salinity, biological activity, depth and many other properties are also important.

### What is soil organic carbon and why does it matter?

Soil organic carbon is the carbon contained in soil organic matter. It is associated with important soil functions including nutrient cycling, aggregation, water retention and biological activity. Soil organic carbon is also relevant to assessments of terrestrial carbon stocks and climate-related questions.

### What does soil pH tell me?

Soil pH describes the acidity or alkalinity of a soil. It affects nutrient availability, microbial processes and the suitability of soil for different crops and vegetation. A pH value should therefore be interpreted in relation to the crop, soil type and other soil properties.

### What are N, P and K?

N, P and K refer to **nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium**, three major plant nutrients. Laboratory measurements can provide information about different forms or pools of these nutrients, but the meaning of a reported value depends on the analytical method used.

### Why can two laboratories give different results for the same soil?

Laboratories may use different sampling preparation procedures, extraction methods, analytical techniques and reporting units. Therefore, results should always be interpreted together with the **analytical method, units and quality-control information**.



# Soil sampling and laboratory analysis

### Can I collect a soil sample myself and send it to a laboratory?

Yes. For many applications, land users can collect their own samples. However, **sampling quality is critical**: a laboratory can only analyse the material it receives, and a poorly collected sample may not represent the field.

The sampling strategy should reflect the purpose of the analysis—for example, agricultural management, research, environmental assessment or engineering.

### How should I take a soil sample for NPK analysis?

This depends on the purpose of the analysis and the laboratory method. As a general principle, avoid sampling immediately after fertilisation or other treatments, define the area being represented, take multiple subsamples rather than relying on a single spot, mix appropriate subsamples into a representative composite sample where appropriate, and clearly record the sampling location, depth and date.

**Always check the laboratory's sampling instructions before collecting the sample**, because requirements differ between analytical methods.

### Can I make a soil sample myself to have NPK determined in a laboratory?

Yes, in many cases. You do not normally need a specialist to physically collect an agricultural soil sample. What matters is that the sample is **representative, uncontaminated, correctly labelled and collected from the appropriate depth**.

For research, regulatory, environmental or engineering applications, however, more formal sampling protocols may be required.

### How much soil do I need to send to a laboratory?

There is no universal amount. The required quantity depends on the laboratory and the analyses requested. Contact the laboratory before sampling and follow its instructions.

### What information should I record when collecting a soil sample?

At minimum, consider recording:

* sampling location/GPS coordinates;
* date;
* sampling depth;
* land use and crop;
* recent fertiliser or amendment applications;
* relevant management history;
* visible soil characteristics;
* and the sampling method.

Photographs can also be useful.

### What is a good laboratory for my soil samples?

There is no single laboratory that is best for every purpose. The appropriate laboratory depends on **where you are, which properties you need analysed, the required analytical standard, cost, sample logistics and the intended use of the results**.

For research or comparative work, it is particularly important to check whether the laboratory uses recognised methods and participates in appropriate quality-assurance or proficiency-testing schemes.

A useful platform feature could eventually be a **directory of soil laboratories**, including location, analytical services, methods, accreditation/quality assurance, sample requirements and contact information.

### Can the platform recommend a laboratory near me?

If you maintain a verified laboratory directory, yes. Otherwise, the platform should avoid implying that a particular laboratory is suitable without evidence of its capabilities and quality assurance.



# Understanding maps, predictions and uncertainty

This section is particularly important for researchers and policy makers.

### Are the soil maps based on measurements or predictions?

Ideally, the answer should be explicit for every dataset. Soil information products may combine **field and laboratory observations, remote sensing, environmental covariates and statistical or machine-learning models**. A map showing a soil property does not necessarily mean that the property was measured at every location.

### What does a predicted soil property mean?

A predicted value is an estimate of the likely soil property at a location based on available observations and information about the environmental conditions. It is **not equivalent to a laboratory measurement taken at that location**.

### How is uncertainty in a prediction determined?

Uncertainty is estimated from the statistical or machine-learning model used to generate the prediction and from the available observations. Depending on the modelling approach, uncertainty may account for factors such as the density and distribution of observations, differences between observed and predicted values, environmental variability and model limitations.

Users should therefore interpret a prediction **together with its uncertainty**, rather than considering the predicted value alone.

### Why is uncertainty higher in some parts of Africa than others?

Prediction uncertainty can be higher where there are fewer observations, where observations are unevenly distributed, where soil and environmental conditions are highly variable, or where the conditions differ substantially from those represented in the training data.

### Does a high-resolution map mean that the information is highly accurate?

**Not necessarily.** Spatial resolution describes the size of the pixels or mapping units; it does not automatically describe the accuracy of the underlying prediction. A map can have a fine spatial resolution while still having substantial uncertainty.

### Why does the map sometimes show values that I don't expect?

Soil properties can vary substantially over short distances. Differences may also result from the depth represented by the map, the analytical method, model assumptions, land-use history or limitations in the observations used to build the prediction.

### Can I use the maps for engineering design?

**Not without appropriate validation.** Continental-scale soil information can be useful for preliminary assessment, regional planning and identifying areas that may warrant further investigation. It should generally **not replace site-specific geotechnical investigation, laboratory testing or professional engineering judgement** for the design of foundations, roads, embankments or other infrastructure.

### Can I use the maps for environmental risk assessment?

They can provide valuable screening-level information, but the suitability depends on the property, spatial scale, uncertainty and decision being made. Regulatory or high-consequence decisions may require field measurements and laboratory analysis.



# Geographic coverage

### Do you have soil data for my country?

The platform should provide a country-level overview and clearly distinguish between **observations, model predictions and areas with limited information**.

### Do you have data about Gabon?

Yes/no should be answered based on the actual datasets available. More importantly, the FAQ could explain **what “data for Gabon” means**: there may be observations from Gabon, predictions covering Gabon, or both, and their density and uncertainty may vary considerably.

### Why is there more soil information available for some countries than others?

Soil observations have historically been collected through different national, research and international programmes. Data availability therefore varies between countries, regions and soil properties. This is an important limitation when developing continental predictions.

### Does the platform contain national soil datasets?

Where national datasets have been contributed or integrated, explain their provenance, licence, resolution and relationship to continental products. It is useful to distinguish **national datasets from harmonised continental products** rather than presenting them as if they were interchangeable.

### Can I contribute my soil data?

**This would be an excellent FAQ if the platform accepts contributions.** Explain who can contribute, what metadata are required, accepted formats, quality-control procedures, licensing, attribution and how contributed observations will be used.



# For researchers and policy makers

### Can I use the data to compare soils between countries?

Yes, but comparisons should account for differences in sampling density, analytical methods, soil depth, mapping methodology and uncertainty. Harmonised datasets are particularly useful for continental comparisons.

### Can I use the data to monitor changes in soil health over time?

Not necessarily from a single static soil map. Detecting change requires **comparable measurements through time**, consistent sampling and analytical methods, and appropriate temporal coverage. A predicted soil map should not automatically be interpreted as a time series.

### What depth does a soil property represent?

This is an important question to make highly visible. Soil properties vary with depth, so users should always check the **depth interval** represented by a dataset before comparing it with a field or laboratory measurement.

### Why do different soil datasets disagree?

Differences may arise from different observations, sampling depths, laboratory methods, spatial resolutions, modelling approaches, definitions of soil properties and uncertainty. Disagreement does not necessarily mean that one dataset is “wrong”.

### Which dataset should I use for my research?

There is no universally best dataset. The appropriate choice depends on the research question, spatial and temporal scale, property, depth, uncertainty, provenance and intended application. The platform should provide sufficient **metadata and documentation to allow users to make this choice themselves**.

### How should I cite a soil map?

Use the dataset's recommended citation and, where applicable, cite the underlying methodology or publication. Include the dataset version and access date where required so that the analysis can be reproduced.

### Can I use the data to develop policy?

Yes, as an evidence source. However, continental soil information should normally be combined with national data, local knowledge, climate, land-use, socio-economic and other relevant information. **The platform provides evidence; it does not make policy decisions.**

### Can these data be used to assess soil degradation?

They can contribute to degradation assessments, but degradation is a process involving changes over time. Reliable assessment generally requires information on **baseline conditions, temporal change and the relevant degradation mechanisms**, rather than relying on a single soil-property map.



# A few "myth-busting" FAQs

These could be particularly effective for a public-facing platform:

### Is the value shown on the map the exact value in my field?

**No.** A mapped value may be a prediction. Check the dataset description to determine whether the value comes from an observation or model prediction and review the associated uncertainty.

### If the map says my soil has high nitrogen, do I need less fertiliser?

**Not necessarily.** The mapped property, the form of nitrogen measured or predicted, crop requirements, management history and other factors all matter. Use the information as one input to your decision-making process.

### Can I determine my soil's quality from one soil property?

**No.** Soil quality is multidimensional. Texture, organic carbon, pH, nutrient availability, structure, salinity, biological properties, depth and other characteristics may all be relevant.

### Is darker soil always better soil?

**No.** Dark colour can be associated with organic matter, but soil colour alone is not a reliable measure of soil quality or fertility.

### Does more clay always mean better soil?

**No.** Clay can increase water- and nutrient-holding capacity, but very clayey soils can also have drainage, compaction, swelling/shrinking or workability constraints.

### Can a phone app replace a soil laboratory?

**For some simple observations, digital tools can be useful.** They generally cannot replace laboratory measurements when accurate quantitative soil-property data are required.

### **What should I do if the soil map and my laboratory result disagree?**

A difference between a mapped prediction and a laboratory result does not necessarily mean that either is incorrect. The map represents an estimate at a particular spatial and soil-depth scale, whereas your laboratory result represents the material collected at a particular location and sampling depth. Differences can also result from sampling procedures, laboratory methods, units, soil heterogeneity and model uncertainty. Check the dataset metadata, sampling depth, analytical method and uncertainty before comparing the two values. If you have high-quality observations, consider contributing them to the soil information system where this is supported.
